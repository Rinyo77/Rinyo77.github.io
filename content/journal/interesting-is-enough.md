AI is probably least likely to go wrong in computer science, because there’s much less human rhetoric, and so much of it comes with built-in verification. After all, so much of it is public.

It’s not even about masters teaching beginners anymore. When top people collaborate with AI, the effect is on another scale entirely.

CS really is a field where, if you’re willing to imagine things and actually try them, you can improve incredibly fast. I’ve only studied compilers for half a year, and I can already solve quite a few problems in relatively difficult CTF competitions. I used to think I’d need four years.

But you could study this stuff for decades and still never finish, because the technology keeps changing. Maybe the point isn’t mastery. Maybe it’s being willing to try a new field, a harder field, and become an elementary-school student starting from zero again.

Security people come in many different kinds: Web, binary, offensive, defensive, software, hardware. Is there a general core metric for evaluating them? I think there is: their understanding of the **Attack Surface**.

Judging purely by coding style, some great hackers would probably score below zero. Yet their code reflects world-class technical ability. In the early 2000s, when most people were only beginning to understand how Windows heap management worked and heap-overflow exploits still succeeded only occasionally, a few geniuses were already making them almost as reliable as stack overflows.

Who working on this stuff isn’t smart? Intelligence is probably the least scarce thing here. They’re all smart, and they all love digging into things.

Data security isn’t anything new either. Humans have been inventing ciphers for a very long time.

Security is important, of course. But... hehe.

I understand why Noam uses examples like that to emphasize the importance of security.

I once tried my own cursed method: using heat to induce bit flips in DDR3. The agent immediately threw Rowhammer at me. There have simply been way too many geniuses throughout history.

Personally, I still think Andres Freund and Florian Weimer are geniuses worthy of respect.

The real world is incredibly complicated.

> Where are the startups that sell access to a realistic simulated internet?

Well, first there needs to be a legal way to simulate a scenario where North Korean hackers break into Bangladesh Bank. The red team would have an absolute blast.

And if the government *weren’t* interested in something this good, that’s when I’d find it strange. So...



If we really care about supply-chain security, I’d actually like to see how governments around the world negotiate with white-hat hackers.

The invisible enemy is the scariest one, so you have to make it visible to the bosses. Fuck, I guess PR really is a valuable profession.

But I think this kind of frenzy is pretty good too. After all, we can add another zero to the budget-request PPT we use to fool the bosses into approving it.

Humans are strange. Whenever I start looking into some random little thing, my friends assume that field must be more profitable. But I’m more like someone who pays money to break my computer, then pays more money and keeps playing.

**What could possibly be more interesting than something interesting?**
